TRADING JOURNALING & REFLECTION

Learn From Your Trading Decisions

Trading improvement requires more than watching charts and tracking profits. It requires understanding your decisions, recognizing recurring behaviors, and learning from your experiences.

WHY IT MATTERS

Journaling Is More Than Recording Results

A winning trade does not automatically mean you made a good decision, and a losing trade does not automatically mean you made a mistake.

A trading journal helps you look beyond the outcome and examine the decisions, habits, and actions that led to it. Over time, this can help you recognize recurring patterns and make more informed decisions.

Recognize Patterns

Identify repeated behaviors such as FOMO, revenge trading, closing winners too early, or moving stop losses.

Evaluate Decisions

Review whether you followed your strategy and risk rules, regardless of whether the trade ended in profit or loss.

Learn From Experience

Use your observations to decide what to repeat, what to improve, and what may not need changing.

BUILD YOUR JOURNAL

What Should You Record?

You don't need to record everything about the market. Focus on the information that helps you understand your decisions, manage risk, and learn from each trade.

01

The Trade Setup

Record the market, setup, timeframe, and reason you entered. What conditions made the trade valid according to your strategy?

02

Entry, Stop Loss, and Target

Record your entry price, planned stop loss, intended target, and the conditions that would invalidate your trade idea.

03

Risk and Result

Record the amount or percentage you planned to risk and the final result. Using percentages or R-multiples can help you compare trades without focusing only on money.

04

Your Emotional State

Note what you felt before, during, and after the trade. Were you calm, afraid, frustrated, excited, impatient, or trying to recover a previous loss?

05

Did You Follow Your Rules?

Record whether you followed your entry, risk, and exit rules. If you broke a rule, identify what happened and why.

06

Screenshots and Notes

When useful, save a chart screenshot and add a short note explaining what you observed. Visual evidence can help you review your decisions later.

Keep It Manageable

Start with the essentials and be consistent. A simple journal you maintain is more useful than a complicated journal you abandon.

TURN RECORDS INTO LESSONS

How to Review a Trade

Recording a trade is only the beginning. The real value of journaling comes from reviewing your decisions, identifying what influenced them, and deciding what you can learn from the experience.

STEP 01

What happened?

Describe the trade objectively. What was your setup? Where did you enter and exit? What was the result? Did the market behave as you expected?

Example

I entered after my setup formed, risked 1% of my account, and the trade hit my stop loss.

STEP 02

Why did I make that decision?

Examine your reasoning and behavior. Did you follow your strategy, react emotionally, rush your entry, or change your plan because of fear or frustration?

Example

My setup was valid, and I followed my rules. The loss was within my planned risk.

STEP 03

What should I repeat or change?

Decide what the experience tells you. Keep following good processes, identify specific mistakes, and avoid changing your strategy based on one outcome.

Example

I followed my plan correctly. I will accept the loss and continue evaluating my strategy over a meaningful sample of trades.

Judge the Decision, Not Just the Result

A losing trade can be a good decision, and a winning trade can be a bad decision. Review whether your actions followed a sound, tested process rather than judging yourself by profit or loss alone.

LEARN THROUGH EXAMPLES

Two Losing Trades, Two Different Lessons

A losing result doesn't tell the whole story. Compare these two situations to see why reviewing your decisions matters more than judging a trade by its outcome alone.

TRADE A

A Loss Within the Plan

Trade result -1R

What happened?

The trader identified a valid setup, entered according to their strategy, placed the planned stop loss, and risked the intended amount. Price reached the stop loss.

What does the journal reveal?

The trader followed the plan. The loss was within the predefined risk, and there is no immediate evidence that a rule was broken.

Lesson

Accept the planned loss. Continue collecting data and evaluate the strategy over a meaningful sample of trades rather than reacting to one result.

TRADE B

A Loss Caused by Breaking Rules

Trade result -2.5R

What happened?

After a previous loss, the trader felt frustrated, entered a setup that did not meet their criteria, and moved the stop loss farther away when price moved against them.

What does the journal reveal?

The trader abandoned their entry rules and increased the planned risk. Frustration may have influenced the decisions.

Lesson

Identify the trigger, follow the predefined risk rules, and use a specific response to frustration, such as stepping away before considering another trade.

What Is the Difference?

Trade A lost money while following the plan. Trade B lost money after the trader broke their rules. The journal helps distinguish normal trading outcomes from behaviors that need to be corrected.

LOOK BEYOND INDIVIDUAL TRADES

Recognize Patterns Over Time

One trade rarely tells you enough about your habits. Reviewing multiple trades can help you identify recurring behaviors, understand what triggers them, and decide whether a change is necessary.

Look for Repeated Behaviors

Review your journal for recurring actions such as entering trades out of FOMO, closing winners early, moving stop losses, or increasing risk after losses.

Identify the Triggers

Ask what tends to happen before the behavior. Does it appear after a losing streak, following a large win, during volatile markets, or when you feel pressure to make money?

Separate Evidence From Assumptions

A few trades may suggest a pattern, but they may not prove one. Review enough relevant examples before concluding that a behavior or strategy needs to change.

Choose One Behavior to Improve

If your records show a repeated problem, define one practical change. For example, stop trading after reaching your predefined daily loss limit.

EXAMPLE OF A REPEATED PATTERN

What if you keep moving your stop loss?

After reviewing several trades, you notice that you tend to move your stop loss farther away when a trade approaches it. You also notice that this often happens when you feel afraid of accepting a loss.

Instead of simply writing "I need more discipline," you can create a specific rule: define the stop loss before entering and never widen it just to avoid realizing a loss.

In future reviews, check whether you followed that rule. This gives you something observable to measure instead of relying on intentions alone.

Remember

The goal is not to find something wrong with every trade. It is to recognize meaningful patterns, preserve behaviors that work, and correct repeated mistakes when the evidence supports doing so.

AVOID THESE HABITS

Common Journaling Mistakes

Keeping a journal does not automatically make you a better trader. What matters is how honestly you record your decisions and how effectively you use that information.

01

Focusing Only on Profit and Loss

Recording only whether a trade won or lost ignores the decisions behind the result.

Instead: Record whether you followed your strategy and risk rules, regardless of the outcome.

02

Writing Vague Notes

Notes like "bad trade" or "I need discipline" don't explain what actually happened or what needs to change.

Instead: Describe the specific behavior, what triggered it, and what you will do differently next time.

03

Changing Your Strategy After Every Loss

A losing trade or short losing streak does not automatically prove that your strategy is failing.

Instead: Collect enough relevant data and evaluate your strategy against its tested rules before making changes.

04

Being Dishonest With Yourself

Leaving out rule violations or blaming every loss on the market prevents you from seeing your own contribution to the outcome.

Instead: Record what actually happened, even when your decisions were emotional or uncomfortable to admit.

05

Never Reviewing Your Records

Recording trades without reviewing them turns journaling into data collection without a clear learning process.

Instead: Set aside time to review your trades, identify recurring behaviors, and choose one specific improvement.

06

Trying to Fix Everything at Once

Trying to correct every mistake immediately can make improvement overwhelming and difficult to measure.

Instead: Focus on one recurring behavior, define a practical rule, and track whether your actions improve over time.

Make Your Journal Useful

Your journal does not need to be perfect or complicated. It needs to be honest, consistent, and useful enough to help you recognize patterns and make better decisions.
